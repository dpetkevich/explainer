How the Electric Stack Works

A camera drone contains a lithium battery, four magnet motors, a bank of transistor switches, and a fifty-cent computer. An EV, a robot arm, and a heat pump run on the same four parts. In 1990 those parts cost roughly 100× what they do today. This paper assembled the receipts: a 99% combined decline, about 12.6% per year, sustained for 35 years. The decline is not a calendar effect. Cost falls with cumulative units built. So whoever builds the units controls what goes electric next. We'll build the drone piece by piece, price it in 1990, and watch that price collapse.

One machine, four parts

We build one concrete machine — a 1 kg camera quadcopter — from scratch: a force law, a motor, a battery, a switch, and a timer. Every electric product is a rearrangement of these four parts, and in 1990 they priced our drone out of existence.

A current in a magnetic field feels a sideways push — no heat required

Our running example is a 1 kg camera quadcopter. Everything it does starts with one force law: a current — moving charge — in a magnetic field gets pushed sideways. Drag the current up and watch the force arrow grow in lockstep; flip the current and the push flips.

A motor is magnets flipping magnets, and the timing does all the work

The sideways push from scene 1 becomes rotation when you wrap the wire into coils and put a magnet on a shaft between them. It only works if you flip the coil polarity at the right instant. Before you touch the timing slider: what happens if the polarity never flips?

A battery forces electrons the long way around — through the motor

Scene 2's coils need current, and the drone's cell supplies it. The electrolyte passes Li⁺ ions but not electrons, so every electron must detour through the motor coil to get home. Toggle charge and discharge and watch the ions rock between the two lattices while the coil spins.

The MOSFET switches the coil current with a voltage and no moving parts

Scene 2's motor needs thousands of well-timed flips per second — push the frequency and watch the mechanical relay's wear counter climb. Toggle to the MOSFET: a gate voltage opens a conduction channel in doped silicon (silicon with trace impurities that set how easily it conducts), and nothing moves at all.

A fifty-cent microcontroller times the flips — that is what makes a quadcopter flyable

A quadcopter steers only by varying its four rotor speeds. So something must decide when each of scene 4's MOSFETs fires — hundreds of times per second, on a hard deadline. Before you drag the loop-rate slider down: how slow can the control loop get before hover fails?

In 1990 the drone's parts cost about $1,000 — and the battery alone was too heavy to fly

The Electric Slide

Part 1 ended at a wall: in 1990 the drone's parts cost about $1,000 and the battery was too heavy to fly. This part shows the law that brought the same parts under $10 — and why that law runs on units built, not on years elapsed.

Cost falls a fixed percentage per doubling of cumulative production, not per year

Wright's law runs on units, not years: C(x)=C0x-b, so cost falls a fixed percentage each time cumulative production x doubles. Here C0 is the starting cost — scene 6's $7,500/kWh — and b sets the drop per doubling. Only while somebody keeps doubling does this compound into the paper's 12.6% annual decline, C(t)≈C0(1-r)t with r=0.126.

One killer product ignites each curve by buying the first volume

Wright's law needs somebody to do the doubling, and for lithium cells that somebody was Sony's 1991 Handycam camcorder. Drag cumulative shipments and ride the cost point down the straight line from $7,500 to $2,010/kWh by 1999. Your drone's cells got cheap because camcorders bought the first billion.

Since 1990 the whole stack has fallen 99% — re-price the drone year by year

Run all four curves at once against scene 6's ledger and drag the year past ~2010. The paper's declines since ~1990:
  • battery packs: −98.7% ($7,500/kWh → ~$50/kWh)
  • motors and actuation: −98.8% ($204/kW → $5/kW)
  • inverters — the banks of MOSFET switches that shape the motor current: −99.5% ($400/kW → ~$2/kW)
  • embedded compute: −99.9% per DMIPS, a standard benchmark unit of compute throughput

Head to head with combustion

Cheap parts explain why electric became possible; physics and system engineering explain why it wins. Here the stack goes up against its two rivals — the combustion engine, and the 'better' battery chemistry — with the paper's real numbers.

Electric drivetrains skip the heat step, so 95% efficiency beats 30%

Toggle the two energy paths — the 1990 nitro-engine RC aircraft versus the drone — and watch where each joule goes. The 95% figure doesn't cheat thermodynamics: the Carnot limit caps only heat engines, and the electric path (scene 1's force law) never turns its energy into heat on the way to motion.

Pack engineering flipped which battery chemistry wins

Scale the drone's cells to a car: drag N to Tesla's 6,831 cells with p, the chance any one cell fails, at 1-in-200,000, and 1-(1-p)N hits 3.34% of cars. Pack builders bet on fewer, bigger cells and won: ~80% of Chinese EVs now run LFP. LFP — lithium iron phosphate — is a cheaper, non-flammable chemistry.

Who owns the curve

Part 2 showed cost falls with cumulative production. That has a sharp consequence: the knowledge that drives the curve is generated wherever the volume is built. This part follows that logic to the paper's ending — why China, which manufactures most of the stack, rationally gives AI away.

The learning behind the curve happens on the factory floor

BYD's Blade pack — long LFP cells built straight into the pack, skipping modules — wasn't invented by the patent holder. A123 owned the LFP patents and died in 2012; BYD, which builds both batteries and cars, saw the pack-level fix. Cut the build-and-measure loop with the toggle and watch improvement flatline: Sony gained 50% energy density within three years of mass production, not before.

China owns energy and action, so it gives intelligence away

Scene 12's conclusion — the manufacturer owns the curve — means China's factories already own two of the three inputs every machine needs: energy (batteries) and action (motors, power electronics). The third is intelligence — deciding what to do: chips, models — and the three are complements, each worth more when the others are cheap, like printers and ink. Drag the price of intelligence toward zero and watch where the profit goes. If you owned the other two, you'd give intelligence away too — hence China's open-weight models, whose trained parameters are published free: strategy, not generosity.
