How SimToolReal Works: One Robot Policy for Every Tool

A robot picks up a hammer it has never seen, spins it upright between its fingers, and drives a peg. That sounds like it needs task-specific training for every tool. SimToolReal shows it needs exactly one skill: move an object to a target pose. Train one network in simulation to push thousands of random fake tools to millions of random poses. Then hammering, sweeping, and pancake-flipping all fall out for free, because each one is just a sequence of poses for the tool to visit. This explainer follows a single 331-gram mallet from 'physically impossible for a standard gripper' to 'handled with no practice on that tool, by one network.'

The basic building blocks

One concrete task anchors everything: pick up a 331 g mallet lying flat on a table, rotate it upright, and strike. We first see why the standard gripper physically can't do it, why trial-and-error learning in simulation is the only viable way to control the hand that can, and why that learning approach then hits a wall: it seems to need a hand-written reward for every task.

A two-jaw gripper physically cannot hold a swinging mallet

Meet the running example: the 331 g mallet from DexToolBench, the paper's benchmark of 24 real tool tasks. Nearly all of its mass sits in the head. Slide the grip force up. The friction torque ceiling τmax=μ·F·r (handle radius r ≈ 1 cm) never reaches the torque a strike puts on the handle, so the mallet twists out of the jaws.

Nobody programs 29 joints — a policy learns them by trial and error in simulation

The hand that can hold the mallet has 22 joints, plus 7 in the arm — 29 in total, far too many to script. One tempting shortcut is to copy a human's hand motion joint by joint; hold that thought, because it returns as a baseline in Part 3. Instead, a policy — a neural network mapping sensed state and a goal to joint position targets — learns the joints by trial and error. 24,576 simulated copies of the robot try actions at once, and gradient updates make reward-earning actions more likely.

Trial-and-error learning needs a score — and every task seems to need its own

The policy only learns what the reward pays for — so what pays for hammering? Flip through the tasks. The reward definition changes every time:
  • hammering rewards impact force at a target point
  • sweeping rewards debris crossing a line
  • pancake-flipping rewards a 180° rotation mid-air
Each task demands its own hand-written reward. Each needs its own training run and its own debugging. That's the wall.

The new technology

The wall falls with one reframing: from the mallet's point of view, hammering is nothing but a sequence of poses to reach. This part builds SimToolReal piece by piece on that idea — the universal goal, the one reward, the fake tools, the deliberately hostile simulation, the deliberately blind observations, and the single human video that supplies goals at test time.

From the tool's point of view, every task is the same task: reach a sequence of poses

Here is the shared quantity the last scene hinted at: the tool's 6D pose — its 3D position plus 3D orientation. Hammering is just the mallet visiting poses g1,g2,...,gK, so the policy trains on exactly one objective: at=πθ(st,ot,φ,g). Its inputs are the robot's own joint readings st (proprioception), the current mallet pose ot, a grasp-region box φ, and one goal pose g. Reach the goal; whenever the pose distance drops below ε = 2 cm, advance to the next.

One reward replaces all task rewards: pay only for new progress toward the goal pose

Now that every task is 'reach pose g,' one reward suffices: rgoal=max(d*-d,0)+Bsucc·��[d<ε]. Here d=maxi‖ot,i-gi‖ is the worst of 4 keypoints attached to the mallet, d* is the best distance achieved so far this episode, and Bsucc=1000 pays once at success. Drag the mallet around and watch: hovering near the goal earns exactly zero — only beating your own record pays.

The policy never trains on a real mallet — random handle-plus-head shapes cover it anyway

The one reward needs objects to practice on — and the answer is not 3D scans of real tools. Hit 'randomize' and generate training objects from a template. Each is a handle (5–30 cm long, 1–4 cm thick, density 300–600 kg/m³) welded to a perpendicular head (1–15 cm × 0.5–12 cm, density 300–2000 kg/m³, up to metal). Then reveal where the real 331 g mallet lands in this space: comfortably inside the cloud.

Training deliberately makes simulation worse than reality, so reality is easy

A policy trained on those primitives in one clean simulated world would still fail on hardware. Real friction, latency, and sensing never match the simulator — the sim-to-real gap. So every episode randomizes the world. Flip the perturbations on:
  • pose estimates corrupted by up to 1 cm and 5°
  • pose readings delayed up to 10 timesteps
  • actions delayed up to 3 timesteps
  • table height shifted ±1 cm
  • random 5 N forces and 0.5 Nm torques yanking the held mallet
A policy that survives all of this treats the real world as one more sample from the distribution.

The policy is nearly blind on purpose: it sees a pose, a box, and remembers the rest

Surviving a noisy world is half the transfer story; the other half is never depending on inputs reality can't provide. The policy receives only proprioception (29 joint positions and velocities, plus fingertip positions), the mallet's 6D pose, the goal pose, and one coarse 3D bounding box over the handle. No images, no mesh, no mass, no friction. An LSTM — a recurrent network that carries memory across timesteps — fills the gap. Watch its estimate of the mallet's heft sharpen after each shove, the way you'd shake an unfamiliar hammer to feel it.

One human video supplies everything the policy needs: a goal sequence and a grasp box

The policy consumes goal poses and a grasp box — so at deployment, one RGB-D video (color plus per-pixel depth) of a human hammering supplies both. Step through the pipeline. First, a 3D mesh is reconstructed from the first frame; the depth map fixes its true metric scale. Second, the handle is segmented from the head to define the grasp box. Third, a pose tracker follows the mallet through the video, yielding the goal sequence g1...gK at 3 Hz. At run time the same tracker feeds the live pose at 30 Hz. Fittingly, this is now the weakest link: 43.7% of real-world failures were tracking losses, not manipulation errors.

Why it beats existing tech

With the full system in hand, we return to the alternatives from Part 1 — copying the human's hand motion, or grasping once and moving only the arm — and watch both fail on a task that requires rotating the tool inside the hand. Then we test whether the generalist gives up anything against policies trained on exactly one task.

Copying the hand or locking the grasp both fail — the tool must rotate inside the hand

Two standard alternatives skip learning entirely. The first is the shortcut shelved in Part 1: kinematic retargeting, which geometrically copies the human's finger and wrist trajectory onto the robot. The second grasps once, then optimizes an arm-only trajectory with the grip fixed. Run both on the same video-derived goals. The tool starts sideways and needs a 90° rotation:
  • retargeting never establishes a stable grasp, because geometry says nothing about forces — 0% success on this task
  • the fixed-grasp planner grabs once, then finds no collision-free arm path that rotates the tool while tracking the goals; it hits the table or misses the poses
SimToolReal rotates the tool between its fingers, decoupling tool orientation from arm orientation. It succeeds where both fail — part of its 37% overall improvement.

The generalist matches each specialist at home — and specialists shatter one step away

The claim to test: the generalist ties each specialist on its own task, and the specialist fails under any change to trajectory or object. The fairest possible rival is a specialist: same architecture, same reward, but trained on exactly one tool and one trajectory. Predict before pressing: does the specialist win on its home task? Press through the three conditions:
  • home task: they tie
  • change only the trajectory: the specialist tracks just the first few lift-off goals
  • change only the object instance (red brush → blue brush): it collapses further
The generalist barely moves across all three.

Why now

The object-centric idea is simple; making it learnable at all took two training-time inventions that only pay off at massive simulation scale. We end with the result that makes the whole recipe scalable: the score on random fake-tool goals predicts real tool-use performance, so improving the cheap objective is the same as improving the robot.

Two training tricks keep learning alive at 24,576 environments — remove either and it collapses

Everything so far assumed the 24,576-environment training run actually learns — at this scale, plain training does not. Two fixes are load-bearing; toggle each off and watch the reward curve collapse (Fig. 8):
  • SAPG splits the environments into blocks of 4,096, each driven by its own policy for exploration diversity. A leader policy learns from all of their experience, with each sample's gradient reweighted to correct for coming from a different policy than the leader. Without this, one policy's experience goes redundant and exploration saturates.
  • The critic — the training-time network that estimates 'how good is this state?' — gets privileged, noise-free simulator state: exact velocities, true pose, grasp status. The actor (the policy itself, the network that acts) sees only noisy, delayed observations, which make value estimation nearly impossible. The critic is discarded before deployment, so no privileged data ever reaches the robot.

Getting better at fake-tool goal-reaching IS getting better at real tool use

The final test of the object-centric bet compares two curves with no obvious reason to agree. One is reward on random poses with fake handle-plus-head objects; the other is task progress on held-out DexToolBench tools the network never trained on, following human-demonstrated trajectories. Scrub the checkpoint slider: they rise in lockstep. Different objects, different goal structure, same curve. The randomized primitive curriculum covers the skill space real tool use draws from — scaling the cheap objective directly buys real capability.
